    Project 2 — Research Project and Paper¶

    45% of the course grade · Solo or in pairs · Research thread from Week 3 (topics out Fri Sep 18) → final paper Fri Dec 11

    What you do¶

    Answer one research question about generative AI in software engineering, and write it up as a workshop-length conference paper — up to 8 pages, not counting references.

    The paper is submission-ready on 11 December. Instructor-approved papers are submitted to a suitable conference workshop (ICSE/FSE workshops, AIware, MSR/EASE tracks — chosen together over the winter break) for +5% bonus credit. The workshop target is deliberate: it is what one semester honestly produces, and a good workshop with engaged reviewers beats a main-track rejection.

    Project 1 gave you a protocol to run. Project 2 asks whether you can write one.

    The research area¶

    Your project lives where this course lives, seen through two lenses:

    • AI-assisted SE research — studying what AI does to software engineering: code LLMs and their failure modes, agents and AI teammates, assistants and the humans using them. This is the subject of your study.
    • AI-assisted empirical SE research — running the study itself the AI-assisted way: AI helps you build the harness, mine the data, and draft the analysis, and your paper honestly reports what the AI did and how you verified it. This is the method of your study. You practice what you investigate.

    Every candidate topic fits the first lens; the second is expected of all of them (it is also this course's AI-use policy applied to research). The course reading list maps the area — mostly SE research, plus the living AI Teammates in SE 3.0 collection and the ICSE 2027 competition track as views of the frontier.

    Team size: one or two students. The rubric is identical either way — scope expectations scale with the number of hands, and the proposal is where scope is agreed. Undergraduates are encouraged — not required — to pair with a graduate student. "Team" below means you, or you and your partner.

    The five steps¶

    Step Weeks What you produce
    0 · Radar 3–5 Topics out Fri Sep 18; research radar due Fri Oct 2 — solo/pair, two directions, three papers
    1 · Question 5–9 Sharpen one direction into a claim; five-minute pitch Mon Oct 26
    2 · Plan 9 A proposal, due Fri Oct 30: method, baseline, what would prove you wrong
    3 · Run 10–13 The experiments — five working weeks, Nov 2 – Dec 4, with the ⚙ advanced-topic sessions alongside
    4 · Write & present 13–14 Draft (Fri Dec 4) → design presentation with preliminary results (Dec 7/9) → final paper (Fri Dec 11)

    There is one formal presentation, and it comes after the draft. You present your design and your preliminary results — so every talk has substance — and the feedback you get — the questions and the discussion in the room — arrives exactly one revision week before the final deadline, when you can still act on it.

    Step 0 · Radar (Weeks 3–5)¶

    Get into research mode early — reading, not committing.

    Fri, Sep 18: ten candidate topics — each a claim with a method and a baseline — circulate on the candidate-topics page (released Fri, Oct 9). Every lecture also ends on an open question, and those are deliberately project-sized.

    Due Fri, Oct 2 — the research radar. Half a page, graded lightly (5% of the project): solo or pair (and with whom) · two candidate directions that interest you · three papers you actually opened, one sentence each on what the paper claims and what it leaves open. That is all. The radar is not a commitment — it is proof you have started reading.

    Why so early: reading the literature takes calendar time, not effort spikes. A student who has skimmed six abstracts by October pitches a sharper question in ten minutes than one who starts fresh in Week 9.

    Step 1 · Question (Weeks 5–9)¶

    Sharpen one radar direction into a claim worth seven weeks.

    Your Phase 3 findings table is the sharpening stone. Red-teaming your own model (Weeks 6–7) is where many of the best questions come from — revisit your radar after it and expect at least one direction to change. That is normal and free.

    The test any question must pass:

    Can you state a claim in one sentence, that could turn out to be false, and test it with the compute and weeks you actually have?

    The commonest failure is a question that would be excellent with a year and a cluster.

    Mon, Oct 26: five-minute pitch to the class. Question, why it matters, how you would test it.

    Step 2 · Plan (Week 9)¶

    Write the proposal using the proposal template. Two to three pages.

    The section that matters most is "what would prove you wrong." If nothing would, you do not yet have a research question — you have a demo.

    Due Friday of Week 9 (Oct 30) — four days after the pitch, so the class's questions are still fresh when you write it. You have been circling this topic since your radar in September; the proposal is the write-up of a decision, not the start of one. (Oct 30 is also Project 1's deadline — both were finished work by then; the Friday is for assembly, not creation.)

    Step 3 · Run (Weeks 10–13)¶

    Do the work — five working weeks, Nov 2 to Dec 4. Three things to know:

    • The ⚙ advanced-topic sessions run alongside (Nov 20 – Dec 2): agentic SE and AI-for-assurance in depth, plus current papers. They are there to sharpen your project in flight, and any of them converts to a clinic when teams need one. Office-hours check-ins are your checkpoints.
    • The proposal is your contract — with one escape hatch. Until Fri Nov 20, a team may re-scope or even replace its claim with instructor approval: one conversation, no penalty. The advanced sessions sometimes reveal a better question than the one you picked; that is what the window is for. After Nov 20 the claim is fixed — a scope cut is always allowed, but a late pivot cannot produce a Dec 4 draft.
    • One rule, from the ⚙ thread: write your evaluation section before you finish the experiments. It routinely reveals that the experiment you planned cannot support the claim you wanted, and mid-November is a much better time to learn that than December.

    Step 4 · Write (Weeks 13–14)¶

    • Fri Dec 4 (end of day): full draft due
    • Mon Dec 7 & Wed Dec 9, in class: design presentation + preliminary results — 8 minutes + 2 of questions. Present the claim, the design, what the data shows so far, and what you will fix before Friday. Every student in the audience is a discussant — questions are expected; discussion is how the class runs
    • Fri Dec 11: final paper + artifact. The week between draft and final is the revision window — the questions from your talk, and the instructor's written feedback on your draft, are the input to it

    What a passing paper has¶

    Not a template — the six things reviewers look for and first papers usually lack:

    1. A one-sentence claim that could be false
    2. A baseline you might lose to. A comparison you were guaranteed to win proves nothing
    3. Variance reported. One run is an anecdote — Project 1 should have made that vivid
    4. Seeds and decoding settings stated. Results nobody can reproduce cannot be believed
    5. Honest threats to validity. Naming your own weakest point before a reviewer does is the strongest move available to a student paper
    6. Contamination addressed, if you use a public benchmark. Not solved — addressed

    Deliverables and weights¶

    Due Deliverable Weight
    Fri, Oct 2 Research radar — solo/pair, two directions, three papers 5
    Mon, Oct 26 Topic pitch 5
    Fri, Oct 30 Proposal 15
    Fri, Dec 4 Full draft 15
    Dec 7 / Dec 9 Design presentation + preliminary results 15
    Fri, Dec 11 Final paper + artifact 45

    Weights are of the Project 2 grade.

    Using AI tools¶

    Encouraged. Two rules:

    • Disclose what you used and for what, in the paper
    • Follow the venue's policy on AI-generated text — read it before you write

    You are accountable for every claim regardless of what drafted it. A fabricated citation is a fabricated citation.
